# THE LONG NOON: How to Build the World

*Methodology for laying out THE LONG NOON's regions, maps, roads and
directions. Adapted from what was worked out building OHMFRONT's first
region (October 2026) and from the research behind it, then rewritten for
THE LONG NOON's world: a sun-killed South Texas, the Thirty-Five north,
night as the living time.*

*Written 2026-10-26 for Mark. Anything not yet decided by Mark is marked
`[PROPOSAL]`. Where this disagrees with a Mark decision in the Complete Story
File, Mark's decision wins.*

---

## 0. What carries over from OHMFRONT, and what flips

| Carries over | Flips or changes for THE LONG NOON |
|---|---|
| **Meaning first:** every region and map gets one line of meaning before anything is built | **Real places are wanted, not banned.** OHMFRONT forbids every real name. THE LONG NOON is built on the real Laredo and I-35, disguised by lost names, with old names found as hints. |
| **Regions as webs of maps**, never a straight line of boxes | **The road is the adventure.** Your pending change: acts aren't organized around cities. The legs between stops are the main content, not connective tissue. |
| **Every road is somewhere:** a name, a look, its own threats, side pockets, forks | **Gates are heat, water, gear and territory,** not field abilities. "You can walk toward the Bleach on day one. You'll die there." |
| **Show, don't tell:** lore you see, never explained | **Night is the default.** Maps are designed for lantern light and moonlight first; daylight is a rare, lethal, expensive state. |
| **Design language is a promise,** kept every time | **Sparsity is a bigger tool here.** The brush country is empty on purpose: "Every landmark is a place people fight over." |
| **Names a tired local would actually say** | **The caravan is the hub.** Home moves with you; camp at Shutter is where the story breathes. |
| **Density rules:** full where people live, open where they don't | **The machines' routines are the mystery itself** (Keepers caring for no one, the Facing at dawn), not only flavor. |
| **Measure scope against real games** | Scope is still undecided for THE LONG NOON, so the benchmarks set the target ranges. |
| **Build it, screenshot it, walk every exit, show Mark** | Same. |

## 1. What the research showed (keep this as the yardstick)

From the ten-game comparison done for OHMFRONT (sources: data disassemblies
and wikis; full table lives in OHMFRONT's `scope-benchmarks.md`):

| Game | Hours | Towns | Dungeons | Maps / rooms | Side quests | NPCs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final Fantasy | 15 | 8 | ~12 | 61 + world map | ~3 | 208 |
| Final Fantasy V | 32–36 | 15 | — | ≤512 slots | 12+ optional | ≤2,276 placements |
| Chrono Trigger | 23 | ~12 | — | ~496 rooms | 6 character quests | — |
| EarthBound | ~25 | ~12 | ~18 | 1 seamless map | very few | ≤1,580 |
| Pokémon Ruby/Sapphire | 35 | 16 | ~21 | **394** | ~8 | ~1,289 |
| **Octopath Traveler** | 60 | **24** | **60** | — | **101** | **741** |
| **Octopath Traveler II** | 60 | ~27 | **85 + 62 roads** | — | 67 | — |
| Sea of Stars | 28 | ~10 | — | — | several systems | — |

**The lessons that shaped the method:**

1. **Octopath's real structure is town → road → town, with dungeons hanging
   off roads.** Octopath II counts **62 roads** as their own places. Roads
   aren't corridors; each has a name, a look, enemies, treasure and usually a
   fork to a cave. That fits your "the journeys are adventures" note.
2. **The good ones are webs, not lines.** Towns have 2–4 ways out, regions
   loop back on themselves, and dead ends are worth reaching.
3. **Maps are many and small, not few and huge.** Ruby/Sapphire has 394 maps
   for a 35-hour game. Most are small: rooms, route segments, cave floors.
   Big feels big because of *how many places*, not how large each one is.
4. **Transitions imply distance.** A quick cut between road segments, a
   bigger moment between regions. The player feels the miles without
   walking every one.
5. **NPC density is what makes a world feel alive.** Octopath's 741 NPCs is
   the best HD-2D figure found. Places feel empty long before they look
   empty.

## 2. The world's structure

### 2.1 The four layers

```
THE THIRTY-FIVE (the spine: the whole route, Laredo → north → back to Houston)
 └─ REGION        a stretch of country with one meaning (e.g. the Ninety Miles)
     └─ SECTION   one map: a stop, a road leg, a side spot, a ruin
         └─ INTERIOR / DUNGEON FLOOR   inside a building, a drain, a quarry level
```

### 2.2 Kinds of section

| Kind | What it is | THE LONG NOON examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hold** (settlement) | People live here. Dense, lit, safe until it isn't. 2–4 exits. | the Span's homesteads, the Waystation, the Schoolhouse, the Cistern |
| **Road leg** | A stretch of the Thirty-Five or a side route. **The main content.** Its own name, threats, events, 1–3 side pockets, often a fork. | the Gate approach, the long flat to the Stumps, the climb to the Foot |
| **Landmark stop** | A small map around one thing everyone navigates by or fights over | the Nut, the Green Stone, the Needle |
| **Side spot** | Off the road, optional, worth reaching | a Keeper Shrine, a wreck, a poisoned well, a dead town |
| **Shade-hold** | A shelter to find, clear and keep; rest and save | a culvert, a rest-stop restroom block, a cellar |
| **Dungeon** | A multi-floor place with a goal | the NIMBUS-3 wreck, the culverts under the Gate, the Quarries, the Fort |
| **Crossing** | The quiet map at a region's edge before the caravan rolls on | the Red River canyon, the last rise before the Glass |

### 2.3 Regions follow the road, not the cities `[PROPOSAL]`

Your pending note says acts aren't organized around cities. So **a region
is a stretch of road and the country around it**, and the city is one hold
inside it, not its center. A first cut, using places already in the Story
File:

| Region (working name) | Real stretch | Meaning (one line) | Holds and stops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Span** | north Laredo | *A town that stayed, and can't keep everyone.* | Mar Farm, the Plaza, the Chapel, the drains, the Old Trail, the White Bowl, the Dead River |
| **The Ninety Miles** | Laredo → Cotulla → Pearsall | *Empty country where every landmark is fought over.* | the Gate, the Stumps and Waystation, the Schoolhouse, the Green Stone, the Nut |
| **The Foot and the Cistern** | Bigfoot → San Antonio | *The land rises, and so does what people will do to belong.* | the Foot, the Cistern, the Walk, the Needle, the Old Mission, the Quarries |
| **The Springs and the Nursery** | San Marcos → Austin | *Green comes back, and the machines are already caring for it.* | the Springs, the Nursery, MERCY's Shrine |
| **The Fort road** | Austin → Killeen | *Halogen's country.* | the Fort |
| **The Fork** | Dallas–Fort Worth | *A city of mirrors that took something from you.* | the Fork's towers |
| **The Crossing and the plains** | Red River → Kansas | *North of the last bridge.* | the Red River, the Tallow Court |
| **The Glass** | the melted plains | *Where the sky was judged.* | the Glass, the Witness |
| **The Last Road** | back south → Houston | *Everything you passed is worse now.* | the Fork again, Seven Flags (optional), the launch site |

That's about 9 regions. If each holds 12–20 sections plus interiors, the
game lands at **150–250 maps**: Octopath-like breadth, in the
Ruby/Sapphire range for map count.

### 2.4 One continuous world, or separate maps? `[PROPOSAL]`

The Story File leaves this open; the Span is already one continuous
192×160 streamed map. Three options:

| Option | How it feels | Fits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. One continuous streamed world** | EarthBound-like; no seams at all | "No invisible walls," the open world | Huge to build and dress at the density bar; long empty stretches turn into filler; heavy on phones |
| **B. Separate maps everywhere** (OHMFRONT's method) | Octopath-like; a quick pixel dissolve between maps, a travel card between regions | Density, quality, phone performance | Less "open"; the road becomes a chain of rooms unless the web is rich |
| **C. Open regions, joined by caravan legs** *(recommended)* | Each region is one open, streamed area like the Span, with interiors and dungeons as separate maps. Between regions, the caravan travels on a **road map** (the moving homestead: pick highway, brush or drains; road events; camp at Shutter). | Your "journeys are adventures," the caravan, the open world, real distances compressed | Each region still needs a strong internal web of stops and routes |

**The owner's decision, 2026-10-29: C, with each region as hub and spokes.** Regions and the caravan legs between
them stay as C has them. Inside a region, a hub (the town or stop) joins its spokes, and every map is loaded on its
own through a transition at its edge (the road fades out, then the dissolve), as B does. Only one map is built at a
time, which keeps phones in budget and loading behind the transition (`docs/architecture/scaling.md`). The Span:
Seven Flags is the hub; the Ramp, the Lake Road (then the White Bowl), the Trail North (then the church) and the mall
are spokes.

**Why C:** it keeps the Span's open feel, makes the road legs a game of
their own (route choice, events, water budget), and stops a 400-mile
interstate from becoming 400 miles of walking. OHMFRONT went the other way
(B). That was right there: OHMFRONT's world is invented, while THE LONG
NOON's power is the real road, felt as distance.

Within a region under C, still think in **sections**: each stop and each
road stretch is designed as its own piece (meaning, signature, routine,
edges) even when they stream together without a cut.

## 3. Grounding it in real places

THE LONG NOON's hook is recognition: "by the time the caravan reaches the big
city, the player knows exactly where they are." The method:

1. **Start from the real map.** For each region, take the real geography
   (satellite and street maps) as the base: the interstate, ring roads, main
   boulevards, rivers and creeks (dry now), reservoirs (dry bowls), rail
   lines, the shape of the town.
2. **Keep the shape, compress the distance.** Keep the street grid, the
   order of things along the road, and which side of the highway things sit
   on. Compress the miles hard, about 10:1 to 30:1 in open country, less in
   towns, so a region crosses in 10–25 minutes on foot.
3. **Keep only the landmarks that survive the premise.** Concrete, steel,
   stone and earthworks survive; anything living, painted, rubber or plastic
   is gone (the Story File's "What's left" table is the filter). A peanut
   statue, a water tower, a checkpoint canopy, a quarry wall: yes. A painted
   billboard: blank. A lawn: dust.
4. **Name everything the way survivors would** (§6). Real names never appear
   on screen except as Old Name hints.
5. **Place the Old Name hints** only where the sun never reached: the
   underside of a fallen sign, inside a locker, a basement plaque, a stone
   face pointing north. One per region at most; make each worth the find.
6. **Keep an anchor list** (survivors' name → real place → hint) for the
   team only. The player discovers it; it's never printed in the game.

## 4. Designing a region (before anything is built)

### 4.1 The four questions

Answer these in order, in a short region bible:

1. **What is this place?** What it was, what it is now, who lives there and
   how (shade, water, the door).
2. **What's going on here?** The story beats, and what the caravan needs.
3. **What's seen but never said?** The lore underneath (§4.2).
4. **What does each section add?** Its signature, its routine, the thread it
   carries.

### 4.2 Threads: lore with a fixed look

Pick a handful of threads that run through the whole road, each with **one
consistent look** the player learns to read. Candidates already in the
Story File `[PROPOSAL]`:

| Thread | The look | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| The Vigil | a lantern lit on a high place; lantern code | safe ground, news traveling, someone keeping watch |
| The Facing | every machine turned east, frozen, at dawn; scuff marks where they pivot | the machines share one mind |
| Keepers | rooms of human belongings arranged with care (Shrines) | machines still caring for people who are gone |
| Noonriders | toll marks and cold-rig gear left behind; claimed shade | Halogen's reach |
| Drovers | slave-line stakes and chain worn into posts | who's been taken through here |
| Given to the sun | bleached cairns on high places | the dead, and how many |
| Staybooks | other families' notebooks in cellars | how others tried and failed to stay |
| Old Names | sun-shaded lettering | the real map under the ruined one |

Threads **escalate** up the road (more Facing marks north of the Fork, more
Shrines toward the Nursery), recur, and pay off. Keep the list short: five
or six.

### 4.3 Every section has a routine

**One machine still doing its old job,** visible in the background. It's
THE LONG NOON's machine mystery made physical: a lawn robot mowing dust (the
Lawnkeeper is exactly this), a sprinkler crawling a dead field, an
irrigation pivot turning at night, a checkpoint scanner still reading
plates, a Keeper sweeping a porch. One prop, a whole question.

### 4.4 Sketch the web

Draw each region as a web before building:

- **Holds** with 2–4 exits each.
- **Road legs** between them, each with a name and 1–3 side pockets.
- **At least one loop** (out on the highway, back through the drains or the
  riverbed).
- **Dead ends worth reaching:** a wreck, a Shrine, a well.
- **One crossing** out to the next region.
- **Route choices** that mirror the caravan's: the highway (fast, exposed),
  the brush (slow, hidden), the drains or riverbeds (shaded, ambush
  country). On foot inside a region, these should be literal alternate
  paths.

### 4.5 Gates: the world says what it needs

No invisible walls. Every limit is something the player can read from a
distance and eventually overcome:

| Gate | How it shows | Overcome by |
|---|---|---|
| Distance from water | the last well, the last dew-catchers; then nothing but heat shimmer | carrying more water; finding wells |
| Daylight | the HUD state; the white sky; the ground's glare | better wraps, Shine, a cold rig |
| The Bleach | bone-white ground, no shadows | a cold rig (the last region) |
| Human territory | toll marks, Noonrider shade claims, Drover stakes | strength, a deal, or a way around |
| A locked hold | shutters down after Shutter; a gate that's "never opened for strangers" | the story, or someone vouching |
| Machine country | Facing marks, patrol paths worn in the dirt | avoiding, outsmarting, or a gun you can't afford to fire |

### 4.6 Design language (readable promises)

Decide early what the player can trust, write it down, and keep it every
time. OHMFRONT locked a door language (a lit lamp at the door = you can
enter; boards = never; chain = later). A version for THE LONG NOON
`[PROPOSAL]`:

| What you see | Means |
|---|---|
| A lantern lit at a doorway or shutter | You can go in (people inside, or a shade-hold) |
| Shutters down, no light | Closed; it may open at Lamp, or for the story |
| A Vigil lantern on a high place | Safe ground nearby; news here |
| Shade-cloth strung and maintained | Someone lives here and will defend it |
| Shade-cloth torn and bleached | Abandoned; salvage, and maybe something moved in |
| A cairn of white bones | The dead, given to the sun; never disturb |
| A trail of footprints in caliche dust | People pass here; so do raiders |
| Machines all facing one way | Don't be here at dawn |

The rule: **never show a promise you don't keep.** A lit door that won't
open teaches the player to distrust every door.

## 5. Building a section

### 5.1 Size

Small and many beats big and few. Rough sizes (1 unit ≈ one person-width,
the Span's scale):

| Kind | Size (units) |
|---|---|
| Road leg | 40–60 long in the direction of travel × 25–35 wide (or one stretch of a streamed region) |
| Hold | 40×30 to 60×45; the Span's 192×160 is a whole region, not one section |
| Landmark stop | 25×20 to 40×30 |
| Interior | 12×10 to 30×20 |
| Dungeon floor | 18×12 to 40×24 |

### 5.2 Every section has

- **One hero landmark,** visible from its entrance (the Nut, the Needle, the
  checkpoint canopy).
- **One routine** (§4.3).
- **One signature** that makes it different from its neighbors, inside a
  region kit it shares with them (palette, fences, plants, materials).
- **Natural edges,** never a wall of trees or a fence on four sides. Use the
  land: berms, caliche bluffs, mesquite thickets, a dry creek bank, the
  steep Dead River banks, a collapsed overpass, a quarry rim. Leave gaps so
  the eye runs past.
- **2–4 ways out** (a hold) or 2–3 (a road leg), and at least one side
  pocket.
- **Something to find** (a Shrine, a staybook page, a wreck, a well, an Old
  Name), on average one per section.
- **People** (in holds, on roads as travelers) with short lines that read
  differently after the reveal.

### 5.3 Day and night states `[PROPOSAL]`

Night is the default and is designed first: lantern pools, moon, deep blue
shadows. Daylight is a separate, rarer state of the same map. Blown-out
white, no shadows under the noon sun, heat shimmer, shutters down, nobody
out. The same place should feel like two places. Gray hours (Lamp and
Shutter) can be a third state, used for crossings and raids.

## 6. Naming

THE LONG NOON already names well (the Nut, the Green Stone, the Foot, the
Stumps). The rules that make those work:

- **Named for what a place does, what it did to people, or who took it.**
  The Gate (it stops you). The Nut ("Left at the Nut"). The Waystation.
- **The tired-local test:** it has to sound natural said by someone who
  lives there, tired, in passing. "We'll camp past the Nut."
- **Plain words, no puns, no attitude.** Mark rejected *the Wide, the
  Upstairs, Dead Air, the Merge* in OHMFRONT for being try-hard. The same
  bar applies.
- **Spanglish where people would use it,** always readable from context.
- **Mistakes are charming and true:** the Old Trail as "the oldest place in
  the world," the Green Stone for a fruit nobody's seen. Survivors guess
  wrong about the old world, confidently.
- **Keep the two games apart:** OHMFRONT also uses *Cistern, Nursery,
  Warden, Keeper, Hale*. Decide which game keeps each before either ships.

## 7. Density and sparsity

### 7.1 Where it's full

Holds are dense: lived-in clutter at walls, corners and work points;
lanterns; people; goats under shade-cloth; laundry; dew-catchers on roofs.
Assign a tier per map (bare, tidy, lived-in, cluttered, squalid, ruined) and
dress to it. Every lived-in map dresses the floor, the objects and the air
(lanterns, strung cloth, moths, dust), not just props on the ground.

### 7.2 Where it's empty, on purpose

The brush country's emptiness is the point. Make it **readable**, not dead:

- **Landmarks you can see for miles** (the Nut, the Needle's lantern) so
  empty land still has direction.
- **Something small every screen or so:** a bleached stump row, a cairn,
  tire ruts, a dead pump, a mesquite thicket. Sparse, but never a blank
  frame.
- **Quiet stretches before big moments,** like the long flat before the
  Gate.
- **The emptiness costs something:** water ticking down in daylight, the
  caravan's upkeep.

### 7.3 Quotas per region `[PROPOSAL]`

| Item | Target per region |
|---|---|
| Sections (maps) | 12–20, plus interiors |
| Holds | 1–3 |
| Road legs | 4–8 |
| Side spots | 3–5 |
| Dungeon | 1 (some regions 2) |
| Named NPCs | 40–80 |
| Side stories | 2–4 (NieR-style, short and tragic) |
| Things to find (Shrines, staybooks, Old Names, wells) | 10–20 |
| Machine routines | one per section |

## 8. Moving between places

| Moving | How it feels `[PROPOSAL]` |
|---|---|
| Within a region | Walking, streamed; no cuts (option C) |
| Into an interior or dungeon | A quick pixel dissolve, a small name tag |
| Between regions | The caravan leg: a road map, a route choice (highway, brush, drains), road events, upkeep, camp at Shutter, then the next region's title card |
| Returning (fast travel) | Only at night, between shade-holds you've found, costing water (already in the Story File) |

The first arrival at a hold gets a title card; every other entry gets just
the name tag.

## 9. Process

1. One line of meaning for the region.
2. The four-question bible.
3. The real-place base map; the compression; the landmark filter.
4. The web: holds, road legs, side spots, dungeon, loop, crossing, route
   choices.
5. Names (as proposals, tired-local test).
6. Threads present here, and how far they've escalated.
7. Per section: hero, routine, signature, density tier, things to find.
8. People and their lines.
9. Gates and design language checked: every promise kept.
10. Build. Screenshot every section, by night and by day. Walk every route
    and exit.
11. Measure: sections, NPCs, things to find, side stories against §7.3 and
    the benchmarks.
12. Show Mark; take his reaction literally; adjust.

## 10. Anti-patterns

| Don't | Do instead |
|---|---|
| A straight corridor from city to city | A web with forks, loops, side pockets, route choices |
| Treat the road as travel between the "real" levels | Make the road the level |
| Walls of brush on every side | Natural edges with gaps; let the eye run far |
| Real names on signs | Survivor names; Old Names only where the sun never reached |
| Explaining the world in dialogue | Show it; people say short, ordinary things |
| Lights that promise nothing | Lanterns mean people; keep every promise |
| Empty land that's just blank | Sparse, but with a landmark in view and something small near |
| Daylight that looks like night with a filter | Design daylight as its own white, shadowless, lethal state |
| A hundred tiny generic stops | Fewer, distinct places, each with a reason to exist |
